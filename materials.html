Philosophy / 03

Wood with
something to say.

Material choices shape the look, feel, stability, and lifespan of a piece. They deserve more than a line item at the bottom of an estimate.

There is no single “best” wood. There is only the right material for a particular use, room, budget, and visual language. Good furniture starts by making that choice honestly.

Hardwood is not one thing.

“Hardwood” describes a broad group of deciduous trees, not a promise that every board is hard or every softwood is weak. Furniture makers often use hardness as one practical reference point, commonly the Janka test, which compares resistance to denting. It is useful, but it is not the whole story. Stability, grain, workability, color, and the intended use matter just as much.

A few materials we return to.

  • Walnut brings rich brown color, a calm grain, and a forgiving feel for desks, cabinets, and heirloom pieces.
  • White oak is strong and durable, with prominent ray fleck when quarter sawn and a character that suits both quiet and expressive designs.
  • Cherry begins lighter and deepens over time, rewarding patience with a warm, even patina.
  • Hard maple is pale, dense, and durable, making it useful where a clean surface and everyday resilience matter.

Grain direction changes the conversation.

Every board has an orientation. Flat- or plain-sawn lumber shows the broad cathedral patterns many people recognize. Quarter-sawn boards reveal straighter grain and can offer improved dimensional stability across a board’s width. Rift-sawn boards create a similarly straight, consistent look with less visible ray fleck in oak. None is automatically superior; each asks for a different design response.

Wood moves because it is wood.

Seasonal humidity changes the moisture held within a board. Across the grain, wood expands and contracts. A responsible design anticipates that motion with appropriate joinery, grain direction, and space where movement is expected. Preventing every change is impossible. Designing for it is the craft.

Solid wood and veneer are different tools.

Solid wood offers depth, repairability, and an edge treatment that reveals the same material through and through. It is often the right choice for a tactile surface or an heirloom component that may be refinished many times. Veneer, however, is not automatically a lesser material. A high-quality veneer over a stable substrate can be an excellent, intentional solution for wide panels, complex grain matching, or designs that benefit from greater stability.

The important distinction is honesty. A well-built veneered panel should be specified as such and chosen for a reason. A thin decorative layer used to disguise a disposable core is a different proposition.

Why we are careful with stain.

Stain can be useful when a project calls for a particular color or when a client is matching an existing interior. But on beautiful hardwood, it can also flatten the natural contrast, obscure figure, and make different boards look uniformly artificial. Whenever possible, we begin with the species itself and use samples, oils, and clear finishes to bring forward what is already there.

The goal is not to make every board look alike. It is to let each material do the work only it can do.
